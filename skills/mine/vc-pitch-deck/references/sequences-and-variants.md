# Sequences & Variants — which deck, in which order, how long

Build only the requested artifact/variant. Slide counts and compression sequences below are optional starting points, not a requirement to produce five artifacts.

Reference for choosing the artifact, its slide order, and its length. There is no universal slide count: the number depends on the artifact (reading vs presented vs demo day) and the stage — the corpus openly disagrees (5–7 Hale · <10 Northflank · 10–12 preuve 2026 · 10–15 YC A · 15 NFX · ~19 pages average in *funded* decks per DocSend · 22–24 Jarvis's "sweet spot" · 9–43 observed in real funded decks, Waveup 2026). Pick by artifact, not by dogma.

## Variant matrix

| Moment | Artifact | Length | Density | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold/warm intro email | Teaser / reading deck | 5–7 up to ~12 slides | Stands alone; more words than the presented version | Never send the full deck cold — it gets forwarded and screenshotted without context (preuve.ai, 2026) |
| First meeting (call or room) | Presentation deck | 10–15 | Headlines only; the founder narrates | One person pitches (YC Series A guide) |
| Demo Day / 5-min stage | Demo-day deck | 5–7 slides for 2:30–5:00 | Near-zero text, huge type | Goal: enter the shortlist, not explain the whole business |
| Post-meeting follow-up | Reading deck + appendix / data room | Unbounded appendix | High | Appendix expands throughout the process |
| Partner meeting | Deck customized to arm the champion | 12–20 | Medium | Give the champion the 30-second internal version they will need |
| Depth beats design | Memo (instead of or alongside the deck) | prose | — | Northflank shipped memo AND deck at seed and let investors choose (Chase Roberts, 2025) |

Jarvis's sharper taxonomy (alexanderjarvis.com): **reading decks** (consumed without the founder — more words AND more slides; several exist: teaser, diligence-support, single-question deep-dive) vs **presentation decks** (graphical, few words, founder carries the load — usually one). Format law: build in PowerPoint/Keynote, **export to PDF**; never send an editable file.

**Leverage note — the teaser is not always right.** Lemkin's counter: a 3-page teaser with no data converts interested investors into passes — "it's sales, lead with your best shot"; when an investor asks for more, send the most detailed deck available. PG's floor: "a lot of the most successful startups we fund never make decks in phase 2," and an investor demanding the deck *before agreeing to meet* is signaling low interest. The artifact depends on relationship stage and leverage — choose deliberately, not by default.

## Canonical sequences

**YC seed** (Aaron Harris, 2018): Title → Problem → Solution → Traction → More metrics → Insights → Business model → Market → Team → Ask. Structural rule: only the title page must be exactly one slide; every other slide is the first of a set — keep n=1 where possible, never n>3. "Nobody cares about your advisors."

**YC Series A "fundraising vertebrae"** (Janelle Tam, YC Series A guide): write the narrative as 10–15 bullets, each backed by its data points; **one bullet = one slide, the bullet is the title, the data is the body**. Order: Title → Traction teaser (1) → Problem (1) → Solution (1) → Traction in depth (a few) → Market (1) → Competition (1) → Vision (1) → Team (1) → Use of funds (1) → Appendix. The traction teaser at slide 2 buys attention for the problem; Vision sits late deliberately — "you've spent 10 slides building credibility; now when you talk about the future they believe you."

**NFX 15-slide** ("The Non-Obvious Guide to Fundraising"): Cover → Team → Problem → Vision → Size of opportunity → Business model → Traction → Product (incl. demo) → Competition → **Path to defensibility** → Plan ahead → **Distribution** → **Secret sauce / key insights** → **Summary of strongest points** → Appendix. Distinctive: defensibility, distribution, secret sauce, and a closing summary — the four sections most templates omit, and the default objections for devtools/AI-infra.

**Sequoia 10, 2026-upgraded** (Waveup, reviewed Jun 2026): the canonical Sequoia sequence omits three slides 2026 investors expect — **traction/social proof, go-to-market, and the ask**; finished real-world decks land at 12–15 once added. Per-slide upgrades: company purpose → bold opportunity statement, not "[X] is a platform for Y"; problem → data-backed headline; solution → one use case solved 10× better; why now → inflection-point narrative (the strongest slide for AI/deep tech); market → bottom-up with a named ICP; competition → durable-moat positioning map; product → engineer a "wow moment"; team → move it up for pre-seed/seed and headline the proof ("we built X at Google").

**Slidebean story arc** (from teardowns of funded decks, 2024): Cover → Team/Investors (only if impressive) → Problem ×2–3 → Status quo → Market → Target audience → Solution → Product → Features → Business model → Traction → Unit economics → Why us. Team slide normally goes at the end but moves to slide 2 when founder or investor names are the strongest asset.

**Demo-day compression** (Kevin Hale, YC): pick the 5–7 ideas to be remembered; ~5–7 slides for 2:30. Three tests per slide: **legible** (readable from the back row), **simple** ("one fold" — one idea that does not fold), **obvious** (a stranger says the idea immediately or the slide fails). Five-minute stage shape (Donna Griffit, 2014): intro + achievement → pain in under a minute ("you meet the villain first") → "we do X for Y by Z" → demo shot from a first-time user's perspective → differentiation, business model, why-now → team (no CVs) → ask.

**Memo-or-deck** (Chase Roberts, ex-Vertex Ventures US → Northflank COO, Jun 2025): "Start with a narrative and work backwards. Whether you write a memo or a deck should depend entirely on what helps you best tell your story." Memo when depth beats design; deck under 10 slides otherwise; shipping both lets investors choose. Also: "your sales deck isn't your fundraising deck."

## Opening: the first five minutes (Sequoia — Aaref Hilaly)

A 60-minute meeting does not buy 60 minutes of attention: "use the first 5 minutes to earn the next 15, which earn the next 30." Three opening slides, in order:

1. **What's changed** — the discontinuous shift that opens the window (open with the change, not the problem: the listener may reject your problem framing before you ever reach the change — NFX/Currier agrees).
2. **What you do** — one sentence, capitalizing on that change.
3. **Fast facts** — founded, headcount, stage/traction, what's being raised.

Then an agenda slide, then **stop and check in** — ask what they want covered, to flush out priors before arguing against them. Plan ~20 minutes of material for the hour (Hilaly; Oren Jacob; Andrew Lee agree independently).

Opening-90-seconds checklist (presented or on a call): sentence 1 = the change in the world; sentences 2–3 = what the company does in nouns a non-user understands; fast facts; agenda; check-in ("anything you specifically want me to cover?"); comprehension check after the description ("does that make sense, or would another example help?" — Seibel). Zero buzzwords, zero preamble, zero defensive hedging.

## Headlines carry the story

Every slide gets one headline that *is* the message — "Don't write 'Problem', write the problem" (Jarvis). Read the headlines in sequence with no bodies: they must still tell the whole story (NFX). Add the conclusion as a caption on every chart ("CliffsNotes right on the slide" — Kevin Hale). Story shape across the deck: the world is a certain way → something happened → the world is a new way (Garry Tan).

## Attention data (date everything)

- **3:44** average investor time per seed deck — DocSend telemetry, **2019–2020 vintage**, still the most-quoted figure.
- **~2:18** — DocSend's live index as cited by Waveup, **Jun 2026**. Attention is shrinking; quote the newer number with the older for context.
- **42%** of decks are never read to completion (DocSend).
- Funded seed decks average **~19 pages**; only **58%** include financial slides (avg 2.3 pages) — and when present, financials are the **most-viewed** pages, so weak numbers are a liability there (Russ Heddleston, DocSend).
- The team slide is the **only slide present in 100%** of funded seed decks (DocSend).
- An inbound reader's first pass is a **15-second down-arrow skim** deciding close-or-continue (Jarvis).
- Presented-pitch attention starts dropping after **~18 minutes** (David S. Rose). Meeting split: pitch 8–10 min, discussion the rest (NFX: 1/3 presenting, 2/3 questions).

## Stage deltas

- **Pre-seed** — the bet is founder, market, validation. Product moves earlier and gets more slides; traction is qualitative (paid pilots, LOIs with scope and price, qualified waitlist, user-interview hit rates); skip the 5-year model — a one-line unit-economics projection does more ("at $29/mo with 2% monthly churn, 1,000 customers = $348K ARR"). Waveup raised a $4M seed with no live product by presenting user-interview research as the traction slide.
- **Seed** — narrative and business model carry the weight; traction turns quantitative; team is the universal slide; financial slides optional (58% include them).
- **Series A** — "harder to raise an A purely on promise." Traction teaser at slide 2, 3+ traction slides; the deck must show that **money — not competence or PMF — is the obstacle**; Vision sized to "how this becomes a $10B company," placed late; growth rate dominates grading (55 of 100 points — SaaStr analyzer, 2026).

## Compression ladder

Build all five artifacts separately — "there's no way to compress an hour of material into a minute and still speak with the same passion" (Oren Jacob, ex-Pixar CTO; build 60–70 slides, cut to 12).

| Length | Artifact | Test |
|---|---|---|
| ~7 s | Elevator line | Listener can repeat it verbatim (NFX) |
| 2 sentences | What it does + how it makes money | Seibel's Airbnb pattern |
| +1 example | One concrete, named, dated scenario | Memorable without the deck |
| 1 / 10 / 60 min | Three separately authored versions | Never a truncation (Jacob) |
| 20 min | Full story, deliverable cold with no slides | Any chart redrawable on a whiteboard in <60s |
