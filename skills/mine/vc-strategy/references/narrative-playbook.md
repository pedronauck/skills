# Narrative Playbook — value proposition and fundraising story

Use only the requested narrative format. A full memo and timed oral test are optional for a full narrative exercise, not prerequisites for a one-liner.

The narrative is strategy, not decoration: "The story is the strategy. If you make your story better, you make the strategy better" (Ben Horowitz). It precedes and outranks the deck — vc-pitch-deck renders what this file constructs.

## Memo before deck

For a requested full narrative exercise, an investor memo can expose gaps before slide design — "writing the prose forces you to fill in the gaps that can remain if you just put bullets on a slide" (Lily Lyman, Underscore VC). Two published templates:

- **Seed memo** (Underscore): problem (Four U's), solution (Three D's), market, why-now, team, evidence.
- **Series A memo** (Underscore — use when the ask is >$5M or the round is priced): adds *Investment Opportunity* (target ownership %, round size, valuation, syndicate, option pool), *Investment Judgement* ("why is this a big opportunity to return the fund **for this firm**" + key challenges), *Key Risks + mitigations*, *Milestones for the round* — split into **value-creating vs de-risking**, and *Vector Financing* (forward cap table including next-round ownership and pool increases).

Qualifier frameworks to run inside the memo:
- **Four U's** (problem): Unworkable (will someone get fired?) · Unavoidable (mandate/regulation) · Urgent (top-3 buyer priority) · Underserved (visible whitespace). Use these as diagnostic lenses, not a minimum-score requirement.
- **Three D's** (solution): Different · Defensible · Disruptive — plus the sharpest question for technical founders: *is there specific expertise required to build this that only this team has?*

## Choose the claim and the point of view — two orthogonal taxonomies

**Claim archetypes** (pick one consciously; mixing produces a hedged pitch): inevitability · disruption · category creation · better mousetrap. Stage-fit rule: a pre-PMF company telling a scale-up narrative loses credibility.

**Point-of-view templates** (NFX/James Currier, 2021 — which voice tells the story):

| Template | What it is | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Industry story | competition, industry change, where you fit | "how an investor looks at the world" — safest default for infra |
| Customer story | one customer finds and experiences the product | strongest with one vivid design partner |
| Hero founder | why this founder specifically | earned only by real founder-market fit |
| Journey to insight | "we tried X, learned Y, now we do B and it's working" | post-pivot; only the 1–2 learnings, never a chronology |

Currier's warning: founders default to Hero Founder "because they like to talk about themselves, not because it's the most effective."

**Category creation is a two-audience decision:** create the category in the *investor* narrative (Sequoia seed partners explicitly hunt "category creators" and "contrarian insight" — "most good ideas start out as controversial before they become obvious," Balkansky); enter a familiar category in *customer* messaging ("the common mistake is trying to be too clever and name a category on day one" — Emily Kramer, First Round 2025: "getting your first customers and getting your first check require two different stories").

## The earned secret

Investors are buying what the team knows that others don't. "Secrets create friends — almost every investor is looking for you to tell them something they don't know"; tell the secret *about the future*, not a descriptive account of the past; the goal is "I want to follow her into the depths of hell because she knows something no one else knows" (Andrew Lee, Initialized, 2020). One acknowledged weakness belongs beside it — concealment converts "is this risk acceptable" into "is this founder candid."

## Why-now construction and test

Build the timing argument, then test it:

1. Which axis changed — customer behaviour, competition, product feasibility, or cost?
2. Is there an external, checkable data point for the change, or only an assertion ("AI is transforming everything")?
3. Is it a market change, or a company milestone in disguise ("we just launched v2")?
4. Answer both "why this year" AND "why this decade" — two different questions (Oren Jacob).
5. If the enabling shift is >2–3 years old, pre-answer "why hasn't someone already won this?"
6. Does the wedge become viable *only* on the far side of the discontinuity? If it worked before, it isn't a why-now.
7. Market timing ≠ fundraising timing (a variable the founder doesn't control).
8. No honest timing argument → cut the claim rather than fake one ("just don't look stupid" — 50Folds).

Devtools template (CRV's Stripe example): name the platform shift → the mismatch it created → the artifact incumbents can't retrofit. Stripe 2010 wasn't "payments are broken" — it was "developers now build on cloud infra and need payment APIs designed for that world, not gateways built for physical terminals."

## Compression and legibility (Kevin Hale's apparatus, YC 2019)

- **Three-nouns test:** after one sentence the listener holds what you make, the problem, and the customer — or the description fails.
- **Reproducibility test:** "can I see what I would have to build?" Ten people building ten different products from the description = fail.
- **Four muddiers** to strip: ambiguity (two readings) · complexity ("a simple idea is one idea that does not fold") · mystery (jargon, indefinite pronouns) · ignorable (marketing/MBA speak — "I will equate with zero information, then I will ignore").
- **Edit procedure:** strip the sentence to its nouns, add back only modifiers that earn their place. Worked: "low-cost, low-power medical devices based on AI and IoT for sub-Saharan communities" → "affordable medical devices for sub-Saharan Africa" (AI/IoT dropped: they're the *how*, and read as buzzwords).
- **Lead with WHAT**, not why or how. Concision is read as a proxy for thinking quality: "if you're efficient with your words, you're probably efficient with your thoughts… and your actions."
- **X-for-Y only when** X is a household-name billion-dollar success, Y visibly wants X's model, and Y is itself huge.
- Anti-defensiveness: founders "give up clarity to make themselves look bigger… it brings me farther away from you." A good one-liner "creates a foundation for curiosity" — it should generate questions, not pre-empt them.

Compression ladder (each authored separately — never truncations of one another): ~7s elevator line → two sentences (what it does + how it makes money) → +1 concrete named example → 1/10/60-minute versions. vc-pitch-deck carries the rehearsal drills.

## Persuasion mechanics

- **Random-sample memory:** listeners retain a random sample of what's said — construct a message that argues the thesis when sampled at any point (a short claim restated in different ways), not a comprehensive tour (Tyler Odean).
- **One weak argument spoils the set** — cut the weakest claim rather than adding a stronger one.
- Speak the investor's metric language: enterprise → LTV/CAC; consumer → cohorts/retention (Andrew Lee).
- Numbers over adjectives: "churn dropped from 4.2%/month to 1.9%/month over six months due to four product changes" — let the listener form the opinion (NFX).
- Buzzword rule: "if a phrase is buzzy but well-defined and germane, it's fine" — everything else weakens (Martin Casado, a16z). Don't posture maturity: "we write checks for teams with little more than a slide deck; over-claiming rarely looks good" and invites heavier diligence.

## Narrative stress-test (the memo passes every item)

1. One-liner passes three-nouns and reproducibility.
2. Claim archetype chosen and sayable out loud; POV template chosen for effectiveness, not comfort.
3. Problem passes ≥2 of the Four U's; the defensibility D is named, with the only-this-team expertise question answered.
4. Why-now passes the 8-point test above.
5. The earned secret is stated as a claim about the future, with its evidence.
6. Slide-agnostic: headlines derived from the memo would tell the whole story in order.
7. Every claim carries its single strongest piece of evidence — and only that.
8. The weakest argument has been deleted, not defended.
9. A partner who met the founder once could retell it accurately in 30 seconds.
10. Narrative matches the stage's evidence class — no scale-up story on validation-stage proof.
11. One acknowledged weakness is present, with its mitigation.
12. Bar test run: told out loud to an uninvolved listener for 15 minutes — lean-in observed, and their repeat-back matched the intent (IDEO via First Round).
