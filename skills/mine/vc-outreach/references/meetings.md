# Meetings — first call through partner meeting, objections, rejection

Adapt preparation and follow-up to the actual meeting. Timers, rehearsal counts, demo lengths, and arrival/response targets below are optional operating heuristics.

The meeting ladder at institutional seed firms: first meeting with the point partner (or associate) → one or two dig-ins → partner meeting. First-to-decision runs 1–2 days to 3–4 weeks. Odds: ~5% after a first 1:1; **25–60% offer rate out of the partner meeting** (First Round, 2024) — the PM is the biggest odds jump in the process. Every meeting where the answer will be no (90%+) still pays out in objections and free consulting — "9 out of 10 VCs won't invest; that means 9 of them end up being free consultants" (NFX).

## First-call prep

1. Research the person (background, boards = what they've *led*, recent posts to reference) and the firm (portfolio — competitor check, stage, fund size, typical check, thesis, LPs). Competitor in portfolio → don't take the meeting.
2. Send the deck or brief **ahead**, so meeting one starts where meeting two would (NFX: "have the second meeting on the first meeting").
3. Decide the ask before walking in — the specific next step for *this* person at *this* stage ("you cannot land a plane by pointing at something that looks like a runway" — Dreamit).
4. Rehearse two lengths — short (survives constant interruption) and long (survives crickets) — and rehearse out-of-order interruptions ("by slide 2 they must ask a question about slide 12" — Oren Jacob).
5. Pre-draft the follow-up email. Test tech; arrive 10–15 minutes early.
6. Qualify them in-call: "Is the firm actively leading seed rounds? Sweet-spot check? Last three deals?" No lead + you need one → defer further meetings until a lead exists (Pillar).

## The 30-minute run sheet

- 0:00–0:05 — warm-up + remind them what the company does (they take 6–12 meetings a day).
- 0:05–0:07 — the rehearsed 2-minute bio: how the founder came to this problem. "The 2-minute bio is more important than the 40-minute pitch deck" (~80% of a seed decision is gut reaction to the people — Pillar).
- 0:07–0:13 — pitch: pain → product → progress → people, most-impressive-first ("you earn every two minutes" — Seibel). Deck on screen; numbers in every sentence.
- 0:13 — **the Magic Question**: "That's the highlights — what would you like to know more about? Market, product, technology, team, customers?" Their answer is their real #1 concern (Beckett).
- 0:13–0:26 — discussion. Ratio ⅓ presenting / ⅔ dialogue; get them talking ("they're convincing themselves to invest — the more they talk, the more they talk themselves into it" — Seibel). If interest spikes on slide 3, skip to it.
- 0:26–0:30 — next steps, always (PG's know-where-you-stand): what happens next · what more do you need · another meeting, about what, when · anything internal, how long. Vague answers → assume the worst.

Demo: in 2026, **live demo** — 7–8 minutes of a 30-minute meeting, no Loom links ("they can't tell if it's real" — SeedToScale panel, 2026); the old never-live-demo rule (Rose, 2008) predates cheap video fakery. Keep the canned fallback loaded.

## Question drills (rehearse aloud; verbatim scripts)

**Trick questions (Brett J. Fox; Pillar):**
- Exit strategy? → "Focused on growing the company; the exit takes care of itself." (Naming an IPO invites "I think we should sell.")
- Why won't Google/OpenAI build this? → **concede they could**, then: cheaper to buy us than build it; refusing to concede blows the answer.
- What if they enter the segment? → focus + execution; their side segment, our sole focus. Never "they won't" or "we'd change strategy."
- What valuation? → "The market will set it." Pressed: "companies like ours have been valued between X and Y." Never negotiate against yourself.
- What keeps you up at night? → 1–2 real risks + mitigations. Never "nothing," never "raising money."

**Process questions (Pillar):**
- How much raising? → approach from below: "We need $X for milestone M; with syndicate support, $Y and milestone N."
- Who else is in the mix? → "Running a robust process, several potential leads, not naming parties" — naming firms backfires in every direction.
- Are insiders participating? → a definite yes/amount, never "they're deciding."
- Why our firm? → the specific partner, the portfolio CEO who introduced you, the thing they wrote.

**Substance:** team experience (pre-scripted humble-brag) · use of funds mapped to milestones · competition across the full package, not one feature · exact best customer · bottom-up TAM · problem size = number × severity · moat with real vocabulary (standards, switching costs, scale that snowballs — shallow answers that get passes: "relationships," "brand," "our culture"). Deep moat/platform-risk answers: vc-strategy's devtools reference.

**Answering under probe (the idea maze):** investors probe to the edge of knowledge, then watch. Confirm the question, answer exactly it, without spin; at the edge say so and deliver within a day. Name the question behind the question ("I think you're asking about X — right?"). When a question isn't understood, stop and ask what they mean and why — answering past it is how pitches die; and never answer a this-or-that question with "both": Posterous's Series A ended when Benchmark's "are you a platform or a network?" got "both" (Garry Tan). Altitude-shift deliberately — 10-year picture down to today's mechanics — the flagged weak spot of technical founders (First Round). Disagree cleanly: "With respect, I have a different opinion — open to yours, but here's what I think." Never defensive — "don't be defensive; every founder has a lot to learn" (Lightspeed).

## Associates — two rules, resolved by who initiated

Inbound associate cold-email = low-priority lead ("I don't know of a single VC investment that began with an associate cold-emailing a startup" — PG). Assigned associate after a real intro = a gate to win, not dodge — partners rely on their one-word read ("forgettable" vs "impressive"), and associates know which partner fits (Pillar). Casado (a16z GP) goes further: junior partners are often the true experts in a space — working through them is generally the right path unless the GP is already known. Watch title inflation: real partners speak for the firm and sit on boards.

## Partner-meeting prep

- Book 30 minutes with the point partner the day before: room context, what to emphasize, founder-vs-business time split, where the partnership will hesitate. They're vouching publicly — treat them as on the team; give them the language and the one-pager (the champion gets ~30 seconds on Monday).
- Diagnose the meeting type by asking: rubber stamp / real decision arena / veto check — "what % of companies that reach the PM get a term sheet?"
- Room facts: ~60 minutes, 5–15 partners, pre-read memo (3–30 pages) built partly from diligence calls with your own customers — know the numbers cold.
- Bring slides + a large appendix (cohorts, revenue by customer, competitive chart, pipeline). Interruptions are a good sign. Pick three points that must land; confirm all three before leaving. Bring energy — the room has had back-to-back meetings.
- A divided room isn't a lost room: "our best investments usually come in very close calls… if all the partners agree it's usually not a good decision" (Komisar, Kleiner Perkins).

## Signals, follow-up, and the ledger

- **Escalating-engagement ladder** (Pillar): questions + data-room opens ≈ 10% likely to fund → backdoor references + customer calls ≈ 25% → full-partnership pitch ≈ 50%. Weight the pipeline by observed behavior, not enthusiasm.
- **The Tuesday clock:** most firms hold Monday partner meetings — a winning meeting produces contact by Tuesday. Nothing by Wednesday → have the referrer probe for impressions.
- Soft-no reading: "investors will deliberately affect more interest than they have to preserve optionality… assume an investor is feigning interest until you get a definite commitment" (PG). Treat every "yes if you find a lead" as a no. Get commitments confirmed in writing; the request itself flushes out the gap.
- **Post-meeting ≤24h** (same day ideal): thank + one line mirroring *their exact phrasing* of the main concern · answers promised in-room, with data · one fresh proof point · restated raise target and timeline · one dated ask. Log the meeting same-day: verbatim objections, questions, stated next step.
- **The appendix loop:** every new tough question becomes an appendix slide — crisp in-room answers next time, and late-arriving investors absorb weeks of Q&A instantly (Pillar). Iterate the deck after every meeting; practice on associates at firms you'd never take money from — "their whole job is to meet with you" (Siroker).
- Mine every meeting with investor-facing questions (NFX): "When you explain this to your partners, how will you describe it?" · "What are the negatives your partners will think of first?" · "What's the one thing I'm underestimating?" · "What traction would make this a no-brainer?" · "Have you seen somebody try this and fail?"

## Objections and rejection

- Weekly ledger review: cluster verbatim objections; separate frequency from severity; fix in the artifact (deck/appendix/FAQ), not the anecdote; re-test on lower-priority investors. Change the pitch only after the same message arrives 2–3 times, never after one.
- **Heterogeneous vs homogeneous no's:** different objections from different investors = targeting/communication problem — keep going with fixes. The *same* objection from well-matched investors = a structural fact about the company — stop pitching and go change the fact (wiki's sharpest diagnostic).
- Pass diagnosis, ordered by base rate: thesis/stage mismatch (most passes — nothing to do with the company) → comprehension failure in minutes 1–2 (fixable clarity) → not venture-investable → no fund-returner path → defensibility unanswered → traction not a trend → team gap → integrity signal → an objection voiced in a room you weren't in → the investor was simply wrong ("VC funds are wrong all the time").
- Never argue with a pass; what continues is evidence. Ask what would change the answer, execute exactly that, return in 3 months with the update — "the message is *we don't give up*" (Beckett). Don't ask investors who passed for intros — it's an anti-recommendation (PG).
- **Stop rules:** ~6 partner meetings with no traction and no fixable feedback → halt immediately ("when you are in a hole, stop digging" — Pillar; a shopped deal "earns a stink") · 30–40 hard no's → stop and re-evaluate timing/targeting/story (DocSend) · "air in the straw" (PG). Pre-warn the team that 5–10 tire-kickers pass before any term sheet, so morale survives.
- Survivorship caveat: the corpus holds no first-hand failed-raise post-mortem; these stop rules plus the Zirtual mechanism — "a soft quarter or so can just kill a round" (Lemkin) — are the guardrails, and mid-raise growth is load-bearing.
- Growth during the raise is the strongest counter-signal: "if you have graphs showing rising revenue month after month, you don't have to worry about any signals" (PG) — raise on 3–4 months of strong growth, keep growing mid-raise.
